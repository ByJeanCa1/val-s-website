Sociolinguistics

Language variation

No two people speak a language in exactly the same way, and no one speaks the same way all the time.

One language, many varieties

Internal variation means that within a single language there are different ways of expressing the same meaning. Sociolinguistics studies how these varieties relate to social structure and to each other.

language variety noun
A cover term for any form of language with systematic features: a whole language like Italian, the speech of a group like New York English, one person's speech, or even how one person talks in one context.
idiolect noun
The variety of a single speaker. Every native speaker has their own.
dialect noun
A variety spoken by a group, with systematic differences in structure or vocabulary from other varieties of the same language.
accent noun
Systematic variation in pronunciation (phonology). Every speaker has one.
speech community noun
A group of people who speak the same dialect, defined by extralinguistic factors such as region, socioeconomic status, age, gender, and ethnicity.

Everyone speaks a dialect. Including you.

Variation isn't random or "bad" language. It follows patterns that linguists can describe, and it happens at every level of structure. Linguistically, no dialect or language is better, more correct, more systematic, or more logical than any other.

Standard dialects and prestige

A standard dialect is a social construct. It's tied to education, institutions, the media, political leadership, and higher socioeconomic groups, not to any linguistic superiority. Standard American English (SAE) isn't even a clearly defined variety; it's an idealization. Nonstandard dialects aren't inferior: they're rule-governed systems too.

Our notes

Someone would probably notice that we say mae and diay a lot, and that we mix English words into our Spanish because we study English every day.

We belong to several speech communities at the same time: Costa Rican Spanish speakers, university students, and English learners. Each one shows up in the way we talk.

Dialect or language?

One way to tell them apart is mutual intelligibility. If speakers of two varieties can understand each other, the varieties are dialects of the same language.

But it doesn't settle every case. Cultural, political, geographic, and historical factors matter too. Mandarin and Cantonese are called dialects of Chinese even though their speakers can't understand each other, while Bosnian, Croatian, Montenegrin, and Serbian are called separate languages even though their speakers can.

A dialect continuum complicates this. In a chain of neighboring dialects, each one is understood by the next, but the dialects at the two ends can't understand each other.

Communicative isolation happens when a group forms a coherent speech community that has little contact with speakers outside it, so its variety can drift apart from others. Today, mobility and mass media make that isolation less common, so speech communities are rarely "pure."

Our notes

Costa Rican Spanish and Spanish from Spain are clearly the same language. The accent and some words change, but we understand each other.

Italian is harder. We understand a lot when we read it, but spoken Italian is much more difficult for us. So mutual intelligibility isn't always all or nothing, and it doesn't always work the same in both directions.

Speech styles

Speech styles are systematic variations based on topic, setting, and addressee. Levels of formality are called registers, and switching between them automatically is style shifting. Choose a style to hear the same meaning shift.

"The film was excellent."

Writing a review for class, or speaking to someone you've just met in a professional setting.

Careful word choice and full forms. This is the formal register.

"The movie was really good."

Talking with classmates after watching it.

Everyday vocabulary. Words like movie, TV, or fridge are common slang: fine in conversation, a little too informal for a job application.

"That movie was fire."

Texting your close friends.

In-group slang belongs to a particular group at a particular time. Like jargon, it keeps insiders together and leaves outsiders out.

jargon noun
Specialized vocabulary of a profession or activity: a variety that differs only in vocabulary. rhinitis, dermatitis, bottom line, hardware, software Some jargon eventually enters everyday language.
slang noun
Stylistic, usually less formal word choices. Common slang is used by almost everyone (TV, fridge). In-group slang belongs to one group.

Our notes

Formal: ¿Podría ayudarme con esta tarea, por favor?
Casual: ¿Me ayuda con la tarea?
In-group: Mae, ¿me echa una mano con esa vara?

Same request, three styles. We switch between them all day without even thinking about it, which is exactly what style shifting means.

Variation at every level

Varieties can differ in sounds, word structure, sentence structure, and vocabulary.

phonetic
Differences in how a sound is physically pronounced, not in which sounds the system has. little as [ˈlɪɾl̩] in casual American speech, or [ˈlɪtʰəl] when trying to sound clearer. Some varieties also pronounce /r/ where others drop it (rhoticity).
phonological
Differences in how speakers organize sounds into a system. Many Americans contrast caught [ɔ] and cot [ɑ] as a minimal pair; in other dialects they're homophones. Northern English dialects may use /ʊ/ for both flood, but, cup and full, good, put. Varieties also differ in which sound sequences they allow: Spanish doesn't start words with /sp/, /st/ or /sk/, so student often becomes [ɛstudɛnt].
morphological
Differences in how words are built. In SAE, -ish attaches to some adjectives and nouns (reddish, childish); other varieties stretch it further (now-ish, George Clooney-ish, close-to-home-ish).
syntactic
Differences in word categories and how words combine into phrases and sentences. Midwestern US varieties may say The crops need watered where SAE says The crops need to be watered. Spanish lets speakers drop the subject when context makes it clear: hablo español or yo hablo español.
lexical
Different words for the same thing, or the same word meaning different things.

Why varieties differ

Regional factors

Languages are spoken in different places, and regional variation follows geographic boundaries: British vs. American English, or the Portuguese of Portugal vs. Brazil. Our group presentation on British and American spelling looks at one example up close.

region
Variation follows geographic location and patterns of interaction.
contact
People in close communication influence each other's speech.
isolation
Mountains, rivers, islands, and other barriers help dialects develop.

Dialectology and isoglosses

Dialectology is the study of regional dialects, often through fieldwork. An isogloss is a line on a map that marks where a particular form is used. When several isoglosses run close together, that bundle suggests a dialect boundary.

Regional dialects in the US

The South

fixin' to for something about to happen; double modals like I might could help; distinctive vowels.

Appalachia

a-prefixing (a-running), some older irregular forms, multiple negation, distinctive vocabulary.

New England

bubbler for a drinking fountain, soda, on line for waiting in line, distinctive pronunciation.

Social factors

Speakers also belong to social groups, which produce social dialects.

socioeconomic status
People use language to show solidarity with their own group or distance from others, and education levels also shape how they speak.
age
Younger and older speakers belong to different social groups and experiences, so they may talk differently. Using like as an interjection (I was, like, going to the store) is associated with younger speakers.
gender
Research looks at how language builds and reproduces gendered identities. The links are cultural and arbitrary: traits like talkativeness or silence are tied to different genders in different cultures.
ethnicity
In multi-ethnic communities, a group's heritage language can shape the sounds, words, and constructions they use in the majority language.
social networks
The communities and groups a speaker actually spends time with.

These factors work together, not one at a time. The same speaker may use different features depending on the group, the setting, the topic, and who's there.

Language and identity

Language exists because people create it and use it every day, so it's a social phenomenon. It sets humans apart, and at the same time the diversity of languages makes it a marker of identity within human society.

Identity isn't something you have. It's something you do.

Most people identify with more than one group: a student in class, an instructor at a summer job, Costa Rican in general but from one specific town in particular. Which identity shows depends on where you are, who you're talking to, and how you want to be seen. It emerges from social interaction.

Language can signal that you belong to a group, or that you don't, and speakers can do it consciously or without noticing.

Stereotypes and perception

Listeners often connect linguistic features with ideas about education, region, ethnicity, or age. But an association is not evidence that a speaker actually has those characteristics. In a 1999 study, John Baugh showed how people inquiring about housing could be treated differently based on the ethnicity listeners heard in their dialect over the phone.

Studying it: the observer's paradox

Researchers want to observe natural speech, but people change how they talk when they know they're being watched or recorded. That's the observer's paradox. No single method captures everything, so linguists combine several.

Case study: Martha's Vineyard

In 1961, William Labov studied speech on Martha's Vineyard, an island off Massachusetts. He looked at the centralization of the diphthongs /aɪ/ and /aʊ/ in words like why and wow. The strongest predictor wasn't just region or social group: it was how much speakers identified with the island.

Our notes

With our families we use usted and speak more calmly. With professors we try to sound careful and formal, especially in English class. With friends it's mae every two words.

It doesn't feel fake. They're just different parts of who we are, which connects with the idea that identity is something you do, not something you have.

Key takeaways

Systematic
Language variation follows patterns; it isn't random.
Everyone has one
Every speaker has an idiolect and an accent, and takes part in dialects and speech communities.
Every level
Variation shows up in sounds, phonology, morphology, syntax, and vocabulary.
Factors interact
Region and social factors work together with situation, style, and register.
Identity
Language signals identity and shapes how speakers are perceived.
Language or dialect
Mutual intelligibility helps, but doesn't fully decide the difference.

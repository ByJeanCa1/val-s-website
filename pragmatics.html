Unit 7

Pragmatics

The same words can mean different things. Context decides.

He is there now.

Who is he? Where is there? When is now? The sentence is perfectly grammatical, but on its own we can't know what it means. Its meaning depends on the context it's used in.

Class video. Assigned up to minute 10:15.

Three kinds of context

Choose a context to see how it shapes the meaning of an utterance.

"Yes."

The words said before an utterance. On its own, yes tells us almost nothing.

After "Do you want to go to the beach on Saturday?", yes means I want to go to the beach on Saturday.

"The professor left."

The situation in which something is said: where we are, who is present, what is happening.

If I'm a student sitting in Professor Sánchez's class right now, the professor most likely means Professor Sánchez.

"Can you take the trash?"

The social relationship between the people speaking.

From a parent to a child, this isn't a question about ability. It's a polite request, and everyone understands it that way because of who is speaking to whom.

Our notes

In Costa Rica, usted is a good example of social context. We use it with professors and grandparents, but also with our parents and even with close friends, so the pronoun alone doesn't tell you how formal a situation is. Usted with a professor is respect; usted with a friend can be affectionate.

Situational context was the easiest type for us to understand, because it happens all the time in class: when someone says "the professor," everyone knows who they mean.
